How to Check If Your Company's Passwords Were Leaked and What to Do Next

Learn how to check if your business passwords appear in known data breaches, which steps to take first, and how to stop the same leak from being used against you.

Published November 16, 2026 · updated November 16, 2026 · 7 min read
15-min assessmentSee plans

Most small businesses find out about a leaked password the hard way: an employee's email starts sending strange invoices, a supplier gets a fake payment request, or someone logs into a shared account from another country. The leak usually happened months earlier, on a website your team signed up for with a work email address.

You do not need a security team to check for this. You need one hour, a short list of accounts, and a clear order of steps. This guide walks you through it.

Why leaked passwords are a small-business problem

A leaked password is not just an embarrassing email issue. When a work email and password appear in a breach, attackers try that same combination on other services, because people reuse passwords. This is called credential stuffing, and OWASP documents it as a common automated attack against web applications.

For a small business, the damage usually shows up in one of three ways:

  • Business email compromise. Someone logs into a real mailbox and sends invoice or wire fraud messages to your customers. We explain the pattern in Business Email Compromise: How Invoice and Wire Fraud Works.
  • Account takeover on tools you depend on. Your domain registrar, hosting panel, payment processor, or social accounts get accessed and changed.
  • Ransomware through remote access. Reused credentials on an exposed remote desktop service give attackers a way in. See RDP exposed to the internet.

The cost is rarely just the cleanup. It is lost workdays, customer trust, and sometimes a legal obligation to notify people. Our article on what a cybersecurity incident really costs a small business breaks that down without hype.

Step 1: Build a short list of accounts that actually matter

Do not try to check every account in the company. Start with the accounts that can cause real damage if someone else gets in. Write them down in a simple spreadsheet:

  1. Email. Every mailbox on your business domain, especially owner, finance, and anyone who approves payments.
  2. Money and payments. Online banking, payment processor, payroll, accounting software, and any portal where bank details can be changed.
  3. Domain and website. Domain registrar, DNS provider, hosting control panel, CMS admin, and e-commerce admin.
  4. Remote access. VPN, remote desktop, and any tool that lets someone control a work computer.
  5. Cloud and file storage. Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Dropbox, or similar, plus any admin account.
  6. Social and messaging. Facebook Business, Instagram, WhatsApp Business, and review platforms.

Mark which accounts use multi-factor authentication (MFA) and which do not. That column alone tells you where your biggest exposure is.

Step 2: Check for leaks using reputable tools

Use tools that are built for this purpose and do not ask you to type a password into a random website.

  • Have I Been Pwned (haveibeenpwned.com). Enter a work email address to see whether it appears in known data breaches. The site also has a password check page that tells you whether a specific password has appeared in a breach, without storing it. You can check a password safely because the tool uses a partial hash, not the password itself.
  • Your email and cloud provider's security center. Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace both show recent sign-ins, locations, devices, and security alerts. Look for logins from countries where you do not operate.
  • Your password manager's breach report. If your team uses a business password manager, most include a report that flags weak, reused, or leaked credentials across the vault.
  • Browser and device warnings. Chrome, Edge, Safari, and most phones warn users when a saved password appears in a known breach. Ask staff to report those warnings instead of dismissing them.

Two practical rules: never enter a password into a breach-check site, and never ask employees to send passwords over email or chat to "verify" them.

Step 3: Act in the right order when you find a match

Finding a match is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to move in a fixed order so nothing gets missed.

  1. Change the password on the affected account immediately. Use a new, long, unique password generated by a password manager. Do not reuse an old one with a number added.
  2. Change it everywhere it was reused. If that password protected email and also the accounting portal, both need new passwords. Assume reuse until proven otherwise.
  3. Turn on MFA on that account. Start with email, banking, domain registrar, and remote access. Our guide on where to turn on MFA first gives the priority order.
  4. Revoke active sessions and app passwords. Changing a password does not always kick out an attacker who is already logged in. In Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, and most admin panels, sign out all sessions and remove connected apps you do not recognize.
  5. Check the account's rules and settings. In email, look for forwarding rules, auto-reply rules, and mailbox delegates you did not create. Attackers use these to keep reading mail after a password change.
  6. Review recent activity for damage. Check sent items, payment details, bank instructions, and admin changes for the past 30 to 90 days.
  7. Document what happened. Date found, accounts affected, actions taken. You will need this if a customer, insurer, or regulator asks.

Step 4: Close the gaps that made the leak useful

Changing one password fixes one account. These steps reduce the chance that the next leaked password becomes an incident:

  • Deploy a business password manager. Shared spreadsheets and sticky notes are the real problem. A password manager gives every account a unique password and makes offboarding possible.
  • Require MFA on all admin and money-related accounts. Prefer an authenticator app or hardware key over SMS where the service allows it.
  • Remove accounts for former employees and unused tools. Old accounts with old passwords are a favorite target.
  • Separate admin accounts from daily-use accounts. The person who reads email should not be logged into the domain registrar as an administrator all day.
  • Harden email authentication. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC make it harder for someone to send email as your domain even after a mailbox is compromised. Follow our step-by-step SPF, DKIM and DMARC guide.
  • Write a one-page response plan. Who calls the bank, who resets accounts, who contacts customers. Small businesses that improvise lose days.

If your business handles customer financial data, check whether the FTC Safeguards Rule applies to you. Our plain-English summary is in The FTC Safeguards Rule for small businesses.

Step 5: Know when to bring in help

Handle a single leaked password internally. Escalate when any of these are true:

  • You see logins or forwarding rules you cannot explain.
  • Money moved, bank details changed, or a customer received a fraudulent invoice from your domain.
  • A remote access tool or server was reachable from the internet with a reused password.
  • Files are encrypted, renamed, or a ransom note appears.
  • You cannot confirm which accounts were accessed.

In those cases, preserve evidence before wiping anything, disconnect affected machines from the network, and contact your bank and insurer. Our incident response service exists for exactly this moment, and email security and phishing protection covers the mailbox side.

Why a one-time check is not enough

Breach data keeps appearing. A check you run today reflects today's known leaks, not next month's. New breaches are published constantly, and credentials from old breaches are still being tried against login pages right now. That is why a password review done once during onboarding goes stale quickly.

Frequently asked questions

Questions and answers

Can I check if my password was leaked without exposing it?

Yes. Use a reputable breach-check service that verifies passwords without storing them, such as the password check on Have I Been Pwned. Never type a real password into a site you do not trust, and never send passwords by email or chat.

An employee's work email shows up in a breach. Do we need to tell customers?

Not automatically. First confirm whether the account was accessed and whether any customer data or payment instructions were exposed. If personal or financial data was involved, check your legal and contractual obligations, including any FTC Safeguards Rule requirements that apply to your business, and consult your lawyer before deciding.

Is changing the password enough?

Usually not. You also need to sign out active sessions, remove unknown connected apps, check for email forwarding rules, and turn on multi-factor authentication. An attacker who is already logged in can stay in after a password change if sessions are not revoked.

How often should we review this?

Review your critical accounts at least quarterly and after any staff departure. Because new breach data is published continuously, ongoing monitoring of your accounts, domain, and exposed services catches problems between reviews.
