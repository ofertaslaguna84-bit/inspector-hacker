The US ground stop was not a hack. What did happen could happen to your business

On November 13, 2026, the United States halted departures for more than four hours. Officials see no sign of a hack: it was a cabling change. Lessons for small businesses.

Published November 15, 2026 · updated November 15, 2026 · 6 min read
15-min assessmentSee plans

On Friday, November 13, 2026, the US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) halted departures nationwide for more than four hours. Social media immediately said the airports had been hacked. So far, officials say there is no sign of that: the network went down during an equipment change. That is the part that matters to your business, because it does not take an attacker to stop you from operating.

What happened, according to what has been reported

The FAA ordered the nationwide ground stop on November 13 because of a communications failure at its air traffic control centers, and lifted it at 8:15 p.m. Eastern, more than four hours later, according to SecurityWeek. Thousands of flights were affected and delays were expected for several days.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy explained the cause identified so far: a contractor was replacing copper cabling with fiber optics at a facility in Arkansas, and the network equipment received more than a thousand connection attempts every four or five seconds. That affected some radars, and the FAA stopped departures as a safety measure, according to Fortune. A second piece of equipment in Texas, updated weeks earlier, started sending data to the network at the same time; why is still under investigation.

Was it a cyberattack?

Based on what is known today, no. Duffy said they have no indication that it was a hack. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said it does not look like a cyberattack, while making clear that they are still reviewing and have not ruled it out yet. The FBI is assisting with the investigation and the official cause has not been announced.

There is a precedent: the nationwide ground stop of January 2023 happened because a contractor deleted files by mistake. That was not an attack either.

The hack that did happen that week

Meanwhile, in Japan, the railway company Keisei reported that someone gained unauthorized access, on the night of November 11, to the reservation system for the Skyliner, the train that connects Tokyo with Narita Airport. Up to 3.37 million records with names, phone numbers and email addresses may have been exposed. Online reservations were suspended with no date for their return, and passengers have to buy from ticket machines or ticket counters.

One case was an internal mistake and the other an intruder. The result for the customer was the same: the service stopped working.

Four lessons for a small or mid-sized business

  1. Changes take down more systems than attackers do. An update, a switch of internet provider or a server migration done during business hours can leave you unable to sell. Do them in off hours, with a written plan for going back to the previous state.
  2. Your vendor is part of your risk. In the flight case, the change was being made by a contractor. Ask whoever provides your support what they are going to touch, when, and what happens if it goes wrong.
  3. Old equipment does not handle surprises. Officials spoke of aging systems that were overwhelmed. A modem, a server or a point-of-sale system without updates fails the same way under an unusual load as under an attack.
  4. Have a way to operate without the system. Keisei kept selling at the ticket counter. Can your business take payments, invoice and serve customers if the system is down for a full day?

How to tell whether it was a failure or an attack

Officials could say early on that they saw no traces of an attack because they had logs to review. Most small businesses do not: when something goes down, nobody can say whether it was a mistake or an intruder. The minimum is to keep the access logs for your email, your server and your admin panel, and to have someone review them when something odd happens.

How long did the ground stop last?
